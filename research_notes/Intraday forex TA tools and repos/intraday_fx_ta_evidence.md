# Published evidence on intraday technical-analysis rules in FX, and design lessons for a pure-TA intraday FX strategy (current to 23 Nov 2026)

Source-type tags used throughout: **[PR]** peer-reviewed journal article; **[WP]** working paper / pre-print version (often of a PR article, used because the full text was readable); **[Thesis]** student thesis; **[Official]** central bank / regulator / BIS; **[Practitioner]** non-academic research with some disclosed method; **[Vendor]** broker, data vendor or affiliate site; **[Own calc]** arithmetic done for these notes on vendor data or on numbers quoted from a cited paper.

Units: 1 basis point (bp) = 0.01%. At EUR/USD near 1.15 (2026 level implied by the range data below), 1 pip is about 0.87 bp and 1 bp is about 1.15 pips. "One-way" cost = cost per side; "round trip" = entry plus exit.

Access note: full texts were read for Hsu-Taylor-Wang (SSRN version), Neely-Weller (St. Louis Fed WP), Breedon-Ranaldo (SNB WP), Krohn-Mueller-Whelan (Bank of Canada WP), Evans (MPRA version), Osler (2000), Hassanniakalager et al. (Glasgow eprint), Dempster et al. (2001) and the Tilburg pairs thesis. For the other papers only abstracts or repository records were reachable (publisher and several repository sites returned HTTP 403/503), and this is flagged where it limits a claim.

## Key question 1. After retail-realistic costs (about 1 to 2 bp per round trip on EUR/USD, more on crosses), which intraday technical rule families have any out-of-sample, snooping-corrected support in FX from 2010 onward, and how big is the net edge per trade?

### Takeaway
No intraday, price-only rule family in major FX pairs was found with all three of (a) a post-2010 sample, (b) a formal data-snooping correction, and (c) positive net-of-cost out-of-sample returns. The rigorous snooping-corrected studies are almost all on daily data, and for developed currencies they show no significant rules since the early 1990s and post-2010 out-of-sample net returns of roughly 1% a year or less; the intraday studies that do exist repeatedly find real predictability at zero cost whose break-even cost (about 0.4 to 3 bp one-way) sits at or below what a trader actually pays.

### Cited Findings

**A. Large snooping-corrected rule universes (daily data, but they set the base rate for developed-market FX)**

- **Hsu, Taylor and Wang (2016), Journal of International Economics 102:188-208 [PR; SSRN 2765673 read in full].** Daily data, 30 currencies vs USD (9 developed, 21 emerging), up to 45 years (1971 to Sept 2015); 21,195 rules (2,835 filter, 12,870 moving average, 1,890 support-resistance, plus channel breakout and oscillator rules); stepwise SPA-type test for data snooping. — [SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf); [journal record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:inecon:v:102:y:2016:i:c:p:188-208)
- HTW cost assumption: one-way cost = one third of the quoted one-month forward bid-ask spread; before Bloomberg data, a flat 5 bp (1970s), 4 bp (1980s), 3 bp (1990s) for developed currencies. Sample averages: just under 4.5 bp one-way for developed and just under 21 bp for emerging currencies. All headline results are net of these costs. — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- HTW full-sample net results: significant rules exist for several developed currencies with net mean excess returns "over 6% per annum with associated Sharpe ratios of around 0.6"; best significant Sharpe ratios 0.652 (NZD), 0.681 (DEM/EUR), 0.752 (JPY). Break-even one-way costs are 6.6 times (CHF) to about 43 times (JPY) the estimated cost for rules chosen on mean return, because the best developed-market rules trade infrequently. — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- HTW sub-periods (stepwise test run separately on 1972-76, 77-81, 82-86, 87-91, 92-96, 97-2001, 2002-07, 2008-15): five of nine developed currencies were predictable in 1972-76; "only a few predictive rules found in the 1980s, and none since the 1992-1996 period". The authors caution that short sub-periods have low power. — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- HTW true out-of-sample (rules selected on data to end-2011, traded 1 Jan 2012 to 11 Sept 2015, net of costs): developed-currency portfolio Sharpe 0.24 and mean excess return 1.35% a year, not statistically different from zero; emerging-currency portfolio Sharpe 1.81 and 7.3% a year (t = 3.5); combined 4.6% a year, Sharpe 1.2. Individual developed-currency rules had out-of-sample Sharpe ratios of about 0.1 to 0.4, one negative (CHF). — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- **Zarrabi, Snaith and Coakley (2017), International Review of Financial Analysis 49:113-127 [PR; abstract and repository record only].** 7,650 rules, six currencies vs USD, Sept 1994 to Dec 2014; data-snooping control by the Barras-Scaillet-Wermers false discovery rate (FDR); costs included. Outperforming rules are re-selected at each month end by FDR net of costs and held the following month. Finding: a large number of rules are profitable out of sample over short horizons (1 to 3 months, measured by Sharpe ratio) but "no rules are consistently profitable over the full sample period"; authors read this as adaptive markets. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v49y2017icp113-127.html); [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18805/)
- **Coakley, Marzano and Nankervis (2016), International Review of Financial Analysis 45:273-282 [PR; abstract only].** 113,148 rules (moving averages plus Bollinger band and RSI rules), 22 currencies vs USD, Oct 1996 to June 2015; Step-SPA test. Best rules reach annualised returns up to 30% before correction; the Step-SPA test produces "a sharp fall" in the number of robust rules; Bollinger band and RSI rules are reported as remaining robustly profitable in the more recent sub-sample. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v45y2016icp273-282.html)
- Zarrabi et al. themselves summarise the earlier Step-SPA literature (Hsu and Taylor 2013 working paper; Coakley et al. 2016) as finding "little or no evidence" of profitability after costs and data snooping. — [Essex repository copy via search extract](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18805/1/1-s2.0-S1057521916301958-main.pdf)
- **Hassanniakalager, Sermpinis and Stasinakis (2021), Journal of Empirical Finance 63:230-251 [PR; eprint read].** The only snooping-corrected study found whose whole sample is post-2010: daily EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY, 2010 to 2016, four rolling exercises (3 years in sample, 1 year out of sample); 7,846 rules (filter, moving average, support-resistance, channel breakout, on-balance volume); Romano-Wolf family test with balancing; cost 3 bp per trade. The test flags 5% to 15% of rules as genuinely profitable in sample, but portfolios of those rules earn "small annualized returns (<= 1% per annum)" and small Sharpe ratios out of sample. Adding Bayesian learning layers (not pure TA) raises this to about 6% a year after costs. — [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/246954/2/246954.pdf)
- **Cialenco and Protopapadakis (2011), Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money 21:176-206 [PR].** This is a daily-data study (not intraday): Alexander filter and double moving-average rules, 14 developed currencies, Jan 1986 to Aug 2009, with actual bid-ask spreads. With spreads, profits and their significance are lower. No White/Hansen-type snooping test is mentioned in the abstract. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:intfin:v:21:y:2011:i:2:p:176-206)

**B. Intraday rule studies (older samples; no formal snooping test unless stated)**

- **Neely and Weller (2003), Journal of International Money and Finance 22:223-237 [PR; St. Louis Fed WP 1999-016 read in full].** Half-hourly USD/DEM, USD/JPY, USD/CHF, GBP/USD in 1996 (training Feb-Mar, selection Apr-May, out-of-sample test Jun-Dec 1996). Genetic-programming rules and an optimised linear forecasting model. Snooping is handled by a strict train/select/test split, not a reality check. — [St. Louis Fed WP](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/1999/1999-016.pdf); [journal DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0261-5606(02)00101-8)
- Neely-Weller out-of-sample numbers (equally weighted GP portfolio, all-day trading): at zero cost, annualised excess returns of 66.9% (DEM), 130.6% (JPY), 127.5% (CHF), 132.3% (GBP) with about 3,800 to 4,900 trades in seven months (about one trade an hour); break-even one-way cost only 0.40, 0.91, 0.77 and 1.01 bp respectively. Rules trained with 1 bp cost break even at 1.5 to 2.8 bp; rules trained with 2 bp cost break even at 2.1 to 3.3 bp and gross returns fall to 6.3% to 31.6% a year. — [St. Louis Fed WP, Table 2](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/1999/1999-016.pdf)
- Neely-Weller conclusion: break-even costs "generally converge to a level close to that faced by a large institutional trader, namely two to three basis points per one-way trade"; when trading is restricted to a 12-hour high-volume window break-even costs fall further; "no evidence of excess returns" once realistic costs and trading hours are imposed, although the rules "discover some remarkably stable patterns". — [St. Louis Fed WP](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/1999/1999-016.pdf)
- **Curcio, Goodhart, Guillaume and Payne (1997), International Journal of Finance and Economics 2:267-280 [PR; abstract only].** Filter rules supplied by technical analysts applied to intra-day FX quotes; the rules, and rules from earlier studies, "would not be profitable" on the intra-day data set. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ijf:ijfiec:v:2:y:1997:i:4:p:267-80)
- **Frömmel and Lampaert (2016), Finance Research Letters 18:177-183 [PR; abstract only].** RUB/USD on the Moscow exchange, several intraday frequencies, a large rule universe (the abstract says more than 80,000; other versions say more than 8,000). Profits "seem much more present on a higher frequency basis", but "the adjustment for real, rather than estimated transaction costs wipes away most of the profits". The working-paper version concludes there is no evidence that simple technical rules consistently generate superior returns after all adjustments. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v18y2016icp177-183.html)
- **Manahov, Hudson and Gebka (2014), Journal of International Financial Markets, Institutions and Money 28:131-157 [PR; abstract only].** One-minute data for EUR/USD, USD/JPY, GBP/USD, AUD/USD, USD/CHF, USD/CAD; strongly typed genetic programming. The abstract reports excess returns that are "statistically and economically significant, even when appropriate transaction costs are taken into account". This is the main positive intraday claim in the peer-reviewed list; the cost level, sample dates and out-of-sample design could not be checked. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:intfin:v:28:y:2014:i:c:p:131-157)
- **Schulmeister (2009), WIFO study "Technical Trading and Trends in the Dollar-Euro Exchange Rate" [WP].** 2,466 moving-average and momentum models on 30-minute dollar-euro data (1999 to 2006) performed worse than the same family on daily data; 2,265 daily models averaged a gross 4.2% a year over 1999-2006. — [WIFO record](https://www.wifo.ac.at/?p=113125)
- **Schulmeister (2008), WIFO WP 325 (yen-dollar, daily, 1976-2007) [WP].** 1,024 moving-average and momentum models were all profitable over the whole period, but profitability on daily data declined from the late 1980s or mid 1990s (versions differ) and "has disappeared since 2000". — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wfo/wpaper/y2008i325.html)
- **Ben Omrane and Van Oppens (CORE DP 2004/35; Empirical Economics) [WP/PR].** Twelve chart patterns (head-and-shoulders, tops/bottoms, triangles etc.) on intra-daily EUR/USD with Monte Carlo significance tests: more than half of detected patterns show significant predictability, but only two imply significant profitability, "which is however too small to cover the transaction costs". — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cor/louvco/2004035.html)
- **Sokolovski (University of Cape Town) [Thesis].** Hourly spot FX rule tested with a Brock-Lakonishok-LeBaron style bootstrap: the rule captures no information beyond a seasonal GARCH(1,1)-MA(1) null model. — [UCT repository](https://open.uct.ac.za/bitstreams/1ca4b008-7ebc-4b87-8804-c8674358c228/download)

**C. Intraday momentum, breakout, mean-reversion and pairs rules**

- **Gao, Han, Li and Zhou (2018)** intraday momentum (first half-hour return predicts last half-hour return) is an equity result: S&P 500 ETF, 1993-2013. — [paper record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/272303487_Intraday_Momentum_The_First_Half-Hour_Return_Predicts_the_Last_Half-Hour_Return)
- **Li, Sakkas and Urquhart (2022), "Intraday time series momentum: global evidence and links to market characteristics" [PR; accepted version read].** 16 developed equity index markets; FX data appear only for converting returns into USD. It is not an FX test. — [Reading repository](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/95566/1/Accepted-Version.pdf)
- **Elaut, Frömmel and Lampaert (2018), Journal of Financial Markets 37:35-51 [PR; abstract only].** The FX analogue: tick data for RUB/USD on the Moscow Interbank Currency Exchange, 2005-2014. First-half-hour return predicts last-half-hour return; attributed to liquidity providers' reluctance to hold positions overnight, not to informed trading; stronger in crises. Evidence is for one exchange-traded emerging pair with a defined open and close. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:37:y:2018:i:c:p:35-51)
- **Zarattini and Aziz (2023, SSRN 4416622) and Zarattini, Barbon and Aziz (2024, SSRN 4729284 / Swiss Finance Institute RP 24-98) [WP].** Opening-range breakout on QQQ (2016-2023) and on 7,000+ US stocks ("stocks in play", 5-minute range; reported net Sharpe 2.81). These are US equity studies. — [SSRN 4416622](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=4416622); [SFI record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/chf/rpseri/rp2498.html)
- **Opening-range / session-range breakouts in FX:** no peer-reviewed or working-paper test of London-open or Asian-range breakouts on spot FX was found. What exists is practitioner material: (i) a SimpleMarkets research log (2026) that pre-registered session hypotheses on BTC/ETH/BNB at 15-minute bars over two independent windows (Aug 2018-Aug 2022, Aug 2022-Aug 2026): gross opening-range edge about +0.04% per trade over roughly 860-880 trades per window, every opening-range cell negative after costs, 41 of 42 net cells negative across four session hypotheses; its "London breakout of Asia range" was positive gross in window 1 and negative in window 2. Not FX. — [SimpleMarkets, Practitioner](https://simplemarkets.io/strategies/research-log/opening-range-breakout-costs). (ii) A vendor write-up reporting that an opening-range breakout on EUR/USD "did not survive out-of-sample" (method not verified). — [Arxum, Vendor](https://arxum.com/opening-range-breakout/). (iii) Vendor pages asserting that the Asian-range London breakout "backtests cleanly" with no cost or out-of-sample detail. — [ForexCracked, Vendor](https://www.forexcracked.com/trading-lab/london-session-breakout/)
- **Hudson, McGroarty and Urquhart (2017), Finance Research Letters 22:136-139 [PR].** High-frequency commodity ETF data (not FX): mean-reversion rules perform increasingly better as sampling frequency rises, trend-following rules deteriorate at higher frequencies. — [Southampton eprint](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/404223/)
- **Hoang (2022), Tilburg University MSc thesis, "Pairs Trading in the Foreign Exchange Rate Markets" [Thesis; read].** 26 FX pairs, 1-hour bars; in sample Jan 2006 to Apr 2015, out of sample Apr 2015 to Nov 2021; cointegration-based formation with threshold signals earned a cumulative net return of 16.48% over the roughly 6.6-year out-of-sample period (about 2.3% a year, own arithmetic), rising to 42.53% after excluding CHF and JPY pairs (an ex-post filter). — [Tilburg thesis](http://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=158611)

**D. Machine-selected rules, genetic programming and learning methods**

- **Dempster, Payne, Romahi and Thompson (2001), IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks 12(4) [PR; read].** GBP/USD intraday bars, Jan 1994 to Jan 1998, moving-window in/out-of-sample; reinforcement learning, genetic algorithm, exact Markov decision solution and a heuristic, all fed popular indicators; slippage levels of 1, 4 and 8 bp tested. "All methods are able to generate significant in-sample and out-of-sample profits when transaction costs are zero"; the genetic algorithm is best at non-zero cost; "none of the methods produce significant profits at realistic transaction costs"; overfitting is a serious risk when in-sample learning is unconstrained. — [Cambridge JBS copy](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/wp0030.pdf)
- **Dempster and Jones (2001), Quantitative Finance 1:397-413 [PR; abstract only].** GBP/USD tick data 1994-1997, genetic programming over indicator rules with periodic re-optimisation: individual indicators were generally loss-making; the best evolved rule was "modestly, but significantly, profitable in the presence of realistic transaction costs". — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:taf:quantf:v:1:y:2001:i:4:p:397-413)
- **Deep learning on indicators (2018-2025):** the papers located forecast direction or price (for example an arXiv 2024 comparison of 21 classifiers on daily EUR/USD, 2013-2022; LSTM models using technical plus fundamental inputs) and do not establish net-of-spread out-of-sample trading profits with snooping control. — [arXiv 2409.04471](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.04471); [MDPI Mathematics 13(9):1472](https://doi.org/10.3390/math13091472); [survey, arXiv 2103.09750](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.09750)

**E. Popular retail methods**

- **ICT / smart money concepts (order blocks, fair value gaps, liquidity sweeps):** no peer-reviewed or SSRN/arXiv test was found; a broker explainer itself states there is no peer-reviewed research showing order blocks or fair value gaps predict returns. — [XS.com, Vendor](https://www.xs.com/en/test-blog/smc-smart-money-concepts-explained/)
- Practitioner/vendor backtests of these concepts disagree and are not verifiable: one mechanical test across 738 assets reports order blocks at -6.5% and fair value gaps at -7.7% average alpha versus buy-and-hold. — [IndicatorEdge, Practitioner](https://indicatoredge.io/smart-money); a broker article reports a filtered liquidity-sweep model at 58.4% wins and +0.64R per trade over "1,000 setups" on EUR/USD, GBP/USD and USD/JPY, but gives no sample dates, no net P&L, no code, and contains internal inconsistencies. — [FXNX, Vendor](https://fxnx.com/en/blog/smart-money-concepts-work-backtest-evidence); a blog test reports what "survives" out of an ICT rule set. — [StatOasis, Practitioner](https://statoasis.com/overfit/research/ict-backtest-what-survives)
- **Fibonacci retracements in FX:** (i) Macalester College honors thesis, five currency pairs, 2003-2008: retracement levels "cannot meaningfully predict" short-term rates; the measured edge was at most about 4% and the author doubted it would cover costs. — [Macalester, Thesis](https://digitalcommons.macalester.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1041&context=economics_honors_projects). (ii) Tilburg thesis on Elliott wave and Fibonacci strategies tested against simulated random walks: profitability "equivocal at best". — [Tilburg, Thesis](http://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=131569). (iii) A 2020 pattern-recognition paper reports forecast-error statistics for GBP/USD retracement levels, not trading returns. — [IJBIDM 17(2), PR](https://www.inderscience.com/info/inarticle.php?artid=108775). (iv) Osler (2000) notes that published support and resistance levels are overwhelmingly round numbers rather than Fibonacci-derived (see Key question 2). — [Osler 2000](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- **Ichimoku in FX:** Deng, Yu, Wei, Yang and Tatsuro (2021), International Journal of Finance and Economics 26:5321-5336 [PR; abstract only]: with default (9, 26, 52) settings some strategies were profitable for stock indices and currencies in sub-period 1 but "failed to create value consistently" in sub-period 2. A later paper summarises it as profitable for stock indices but not currencies. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:ijfiec:v:26:y:2021:i:4:p:5321-5336)
- **Candlesticks in FX:** Orquín-Serrano (2020), Mathematics 8(5):802 [PR; abstract-level]: classification of single-candlestick patterns on EUR/USD; "no net positive average returns are found in any case after taking into account transaction costs". — [MDPI](https://mdpi.com/2227-7390/8/5/802)
- **Harmonic patterns, supply and demand zones, Elliott wave (beyond the thesis above):** no formal evidence found.

### Inferences
- The only post-2010, snooping-corrected, out-of-sample numbers for major pairs are daily-bar numbers: about 1.35% a year net (Sharpe 0.24, insignificant) for developed currencies in 2012-2015 (HTW) and at most 1% a year net for EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/JPY in 2013-2016 (Hassanniakalager et al.). Converted to a per-trade figure these are fractions of a basis point to a few basis points per trade depending on turnover, that is, of the same order as a retail round-trip cost. There is no comparable intraday number to report.
- The most consistent pattern across intraday studies (Neely-Weller; Dempster et al.; Curcio et al.; Frömmel-Lampaert; Ben Omrane-Van Oppens) is "predictability yes, profit after costs no". Read as a design prior: gross edge per trade for a mechanically selected intraday rule on a major pair has historically been about 0.5 to 3 bp, so a strategy needs either a cost well under 1 bp per side or a much larger move per trade (fewer, longer trades).
- Positive intraday claims (Manahov et al. 2014; Dempster-Jones 2001; the Tilburg pairs thesis; Coakley et al.'s Bollinger/RSI result) share weaknesses for present purposes: old or single samples, cost assumptions not verified here, model selection after the fact, or no family-wise snooping test over everything tried. They justify testing those families, not trusting them.
- If a ranking of "worth testing first" is wanted, the evidence supports this order: (1) session/time-of-day structure used as a filter or as the trade itself with one or two trades a day (documented, snooping-robust in the sense of being replicated across 20 years and data sets, though small; see Key question 2); (2) mean-reversion at intraday horizons, including Bollinger/RSI-type rules and range-bound crosses (indirect support from Coakley et al., Hudson et al., and the fix reversal literature); (3) hourly cointegration pairs across related currencies (one thesis, modest net return); (4) session-range breakouts (no formal FX evidence either way; the one disciplined practitioner test in another asset class found the gross edge smaller than costs). Retail pattern methods (ICT/SMC, harmonics, Elliott, Fibonacci, candlesticks, Ichimoku) come last: either untested or tested with null results after costs.

### Gaps
- Net-of-cost Sharpe ratios and per-rule tables for Zarrabi-Snaith-Coakley (2017) and Coakley-Marzano-Nankervis (2016) were not retrieved (repository returned 503 / TLS errors; publisher paywalled). The data frequency of Zarrabi et al. is not stated in the abstract; it is believed to be daily but this was not confirmed.
- Manahov, Hudson and Gebka (2014): sample dates, cost per trade and out-of-sample protocol not verified; the positive claim rests on the abstract.
- Kozhan and Salmon (2012, Journal of Financial Markets 15:1-28, "The information content of a limit order book: the case of an FX market"): only the abstract-level framing was retrieved (whether order book information adds economic value in a simple trading scheme, using a linear model and a genetic algorithm). Its results by year could not be confirmed in this session. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v15y2012i1p1-28.html)
- Marshall, Young and Rose (2006), "Candlestick technical trading strategies: can they create value for investors?" was not retrieved. From prior knowledge it is an equity study (Dow Jones component stocks) with a null result; treat as unverified here. No FX-specific bootstrap candlestick study on intraday bars other than Orquín-Serrano (2020) was found.
- No study from 2018-2026 was found that applies White's reality check, Hansen's SPA, Romano-Wolf or FDR to 1-minute-to-hourly rules on major spot FX pairs with bid/ask data. This appears to be a genuine hole in the literature rather than a search failure, but that cannot be proven.
- No formal test was found of short-horizon reversal after large intraday moves in major FX pairs, other than the fix-window reversals in Key question 2.
- Elaut et al. (2018): regression coefficients, R-squared and any net-of-cost strategy return were not retrieved.

## Key question 2. Which effects are real but too small for retail (for example time-of-day drift measured in fractions of a basis point), and which are simply absent?

### Takeaway
Time-of-day and fixing effects are the best-documented intraday regularities in FX (about 2.5 to 4 bp per window per day gross, replicated over 20 years and several data sets), but they are consumed by a full quoted spread and only survive at interbank-level or half-spread costs, and they have weakened since about 2013. Round-number order clustering and published support/resistance levels have real but modest predictive content for where trends pause; intraday momentum of the equity type and the retail pattern methods have no supporting FX evidence.

### Cited Findings

**Real, replicated, but small**

- **Breedon and Ranaldo (2013), Journal of Money, Credit and Banking 45:953-965 [PR; SNB WP 2011-04 read].** EBS interdealer firm quotes and trades, Jan 1997 to June 2007; EUR/USD, USD/JPY, EUR/JPY, GBP/USD, USD/CHF, AUD/USD. Currencies depreciate in their own trading hours. Mid-quote annualised log returns: EUR/USD -8.4% in the EUR session and +10.0% in the USD session; GBP/USD -7.1% (GBP session) and +9.2% (USD session); USD/CHF +9.5% (CHF session) and -8.8% (USD session); AUD/USD -2.8% and +1.6%; USD/JPY +1.7% and 0.0%; EUR/JPY +2.9% and -5.7%. — [SNB WP PDF, Table 2](https://www.snb.ch/public/asset/en/www-snb-ch/publications/research/working-papers/2011/working_paper_2011_04/publications0_en/working_paper_2011_04.n.pdf); [journal record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:jmoncb:v:45:y:2013:i:5:p:953-965)
- Breedon-Ranaldo net of costs (trading at EBS firm bid and ask, short the base currency in its own session and long in the counter-currency session): EUR/USD +6% and +7% a year (Sharpe 1.3 for the morning short, 0.9 for the afternoon long); every other pair negative: USD/JPY -13% and -5%, EUR/JPY -5% and -42%, GBP/USD -12% and -8%, USD/CHF -8% and -2%, AUD/USD -51% and -50%. "Most of these simple time-of-day trading strategies are not profitable when trading costs are included." The EUR/USD session gap was stable year by year from 1999 to 2007 except 2004. — [SNB WP PDF](https://www.snb.ch/public/asset/en/www-snb-ch/publications/research/working-papers/2011/working_paper_2011_04/publications0_en/working_paper_2011_04.n.pdf)
- **Ranaldo (2009), Journal of Banking and Finance 33:2199-2206 [PR; abstract only].** Earlier documentation of the same pattern: domestic currencies fall during domestic working hours and rise during foreign working hours, across several exchange rates and years, robust to calendar effects; explained by domestic agents being net buyers of foreign currency during their own hours. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jbfina:v:33:y:2009:i:12:p:2199-2206)
- **Krohn, Mueller and Whelan (2024), Journal of Finance 79:541-578 [PR; Bank of Canada SWP 2021-48 read].** G9 currencies vs USD, Jan 1999 to Dec 2019, 5-minute indicative quotes plus firm interdealer and CME futures data. The USD appreciates before each of the Tokyo, ECB/Frankfurt and London fixes and depreciates after, tracing a W shape over 24 hours. Magnitude: about 2.5 bp USD appreciation before each fix and about the same reversal after, "on a daily basis"; t-statistics 4.5 to 11.7; dollar-portfolio window returns about 4.2% to 4.6% a year around the European fixes and about 6.5% annualised on either side of the fix in 1999-2014. — [Bank of Canada WP](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf); [journal record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:79:y:2024:i:1:p:541-578)
- Krohn et al. gross strategy returns (no costs): Tokyo-fix reversal 6.2% (CHF) to 14.2% (NZD) a year; European window 15.6% (EUR), 12.4% (GBP), 12% (CHF); annualised standard deviation generally below 8%. — [Bank of Canada WP](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- Krohn et al. net of costs (Table IX; buy at ask, sell at bid; windows: long USD 5:00-8:55 pm then short USD 8:55 pm-2:00 am New York time around Tokyo; long USD 2:00-8:15 am, short USD 11:00 am-5:00 pm around Europe). Annualised return / Sharpe for pre+post combined:
  - Tokyo, full quoted spread: EUR -5.14% / -1.66; GBP -6.92% / -2.19; JPY -6.54% / -1.42. Half spread: EUR +2.65% / 0.39; GBP +0.51% / -0.18; JPY +2.73% / 0.29. Zero spread: EUR 10.44% / 2.45; GBP 7.94% / 1.84; JPY 12.00% / 1.99.
  - Europe, full quoted spread: EUR +1.52% / 0.05; GBP -1.51% / -0.38; JPY -18.23% / -2.86. Half spread: EUR +8.64% / 1.03; GBP +5.47% / 0.60; JPY -9.27% / -1.54. Zero spread: EUR 15.75% / 2.01; GBP 12.45% / 1.57; JPY -0.31% / -0.22.
  — [Bank of Canada WP, Table IX](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- Krohn et al.'s own verdict: "it is not obvious that this can be exploited by the average trader"; returns from a narrow window around the fix "are usually more than offset by transaction costs"; holding through the longer drift window may be profitable "for traders that are able to get reasonably good conditions". They cite evidence that indicative quoted spreads overstate effective institutional spreads by up to 75%. — [Bank of Canada WP](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- Krohn et al. also find the pattern in firm interdealer quotes, but order flow in CME FX futures "displays no discernible pattern over the course of the day" and pre-fix futures order flow does not predict later returns; dealer-platform order flow is the informative one. — [Bank of Canada WP](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- **Evans (2018), Journal of Banking and Finance 87:233-247 [PR; MPRA 81583 read].** 21 currency pairs, start of 2004 to end of 2013, mid-quotes from Gain Capital (parent of Forex.com) with EBS spread samples from 2007, 2010, 2013. Price changes around the 4 pm London WM/Reuters fix show extraordinary volatility and negative serial correlation, especially on the last trading day of the month. On ordinary days there are "small reversals during the first minute after the Fix (on the order of one basis point)". — [MPRA paper](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81583/1/MPRA_paper_81583.pdf)
- Evans end-of-month reversal strategy (position opened at the fix against the pre-fix move, held 1, 5 or 15 minutes; cost assumed zero at the fix and half the normal EBS inside spread at exit): returns positive at some horizon for all pairs but JPY/USD and USD/GBP; reported annualised Sharpe ratios above 1 for at least one horizon in 15 of 21 pairs and above 2 in eight. Examples at the 15-minute horizon (annual % return / Sharpe): EUR/USD 3.97 / 1.88; AUD/USD 9.19 / 3.38; EUR/GBP 8.33 / 2.87; USD/GBP -3.05 / -1.18; JPY/USD -1.65 / -0.75. Out of sample, choosing each pair's best pre-2010 horizon and trading post-2010 gave an average return of 3.25% across currencies. — [MPRA paper, Table 6](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81583/1/MPRA_paper_81583.pdf)
- Evans notes that the retail aggregator's quoted spread "is roughly twice as large as the inside spreads" on EBS and Reuters, and his net results assume the interbank spread. — [MPRA paper, footnote 18](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81583/1/MPRA_paper_81583.pdf)
- **Melvin and Prins (2015), Journal of Financial Markets 22:50-72 [PR; abstract only].** Equity-market performance over the month predicts currency moves into the month-end 4 pm London fix, consistent with hedge rebalancing by international equity managers. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:22:y:2015:i:c:p:50-72)
- **Osler (2000), FRBNY Economic Policy Review 6(2) [Official/PR].** Support and resistance levels published daily by six firms, 1996-1998, tested on 1-minute indicative quotes 9 am-4 pm New York for USD/DEM, USD/JPY, GBP/USD against artificial levels (bootstrap). The published levels predicted intraday trend interruptions better than arbitrary levels; power varied by firm and pair (better for yen and pound than mark) and lasted at least five business days. Bounce frequencies (Table 8), artificial levels versus the six firms' published levels: German mark 54.9% versus 56.6% to 62.0%; Japanese yen 57.3% versus 60.7% to 66.5%; British pound 56.3% versus 58.8% to 63.0%. Five trading days after publication the gap narrows but persists (mark 53.8% versus 55.5% to 59.0%; yen 55.4% versus 59.5% to 64.3%; pound 54.0% versus 56.5% to 59.0%). More than 70% of published levels ended in 0 and 96% in 0 or 5. This is a predictability test; no trading profits net of costs are reported. — [FRBNY EPR](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf)
- **Osler (2003), Journal of Finance 58:1791-1819 [PR; FRBNY Staff Report 125].** About 9,700 stop-loss and take-profit orders at a large dealing bank, Sept 1999 to April 2000. Take-profit orders cluster at round numbers (so trends tend to reverse there); stop-loss orders cluster just beyond round numbers (so trends accelerate once the level breaks). — [FRBNY SR 125](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr125.pdf); [journal record](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:58:y:2003:i:5:p:1791-1819)
- **Osler (2005, Journal of International Money and Finance; FRBNY Staff Report 150) [PR].** Stop-loss orders contribute to price cascades: executed stop-loss sells cluster just below round numbers and stop-loss buys just above. — [FRBNY SR 150](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr150.pdf)

**Absent or unsupported in FX**

- Equity-style intraday momentum (first half hour predicts last half hour) has FX support only for RUB/USD on the Moscow exchange (Elaut et al. 2018); no test on a 24-hour major pair was found. — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:finmar:v:37:y:2018:i:c:p:35-51)
- An event study on 1-minute EUR/USD data (2008-2016) around ECB communication found no systematic intraday drift. — [Czech Journal of Economics and Finance 76(2), 2026, PR](https://ideas.repec.org/a/fau/fauart/v76y2026i2p191-228.html)
- Chart patterns, single candlesticks, Fibonacci levels and Ichimoku: tests that exist find predictability too small for costs or no consistent value (see Key question 1, section E).

### Inferences
- [Own calc] Per-day size of the time-of-day effect: 8.4% a year over about 252 days is about 3.3 bp per EUR session; 10.0% is about 4.0 bp per USD session; Krohn et al. give about 2.5 bp per fix window. A retail round trip of 1 to 2 bp therefore takes roughly 25% to 80% of the gross effect per window, before any decay since the sample ended.
- [Own calc] Breedon-Ranaldo's EUR/USD gross-to-net gap (8.4% to 6%, 10.0% to 7%) implies the EBS interdealer round-trip cost was about 0.95 to 1.2 bp per daily trade in 1997-2007. A retail client paying a similar all-in cost today (for example an agency broker with a 0.1 to 0.2 pip spread plus commission) is in roughly the position of the 1997-2007 interdealer trader, for EUR/USD only.
- The Krohn half-spread results suggest the practical retail question is cost, not existence: at half the quoted spread the Europe-window EUR and GBP trades were positive (Sharpe 1.03 and 0.60 over 1999-2019), at the full quoted spread they were about zero or negative. The pattern failing in CME futures order flow is a caution against assuming it transfers cleanly to FX futures.
- Round-number clustering is the one microstructure fact that supports a pure price rule mechanically: fade into a round number (take-profit cluster), follow through after a clean break (stop cluster). The measured effect is roughly 2 to 9 percentage points of bounce frequency over arbitrary levels, so it is better used as a filter on entries and stop placement than as a standalone signal.
- The month-end fix reversal is real in 2004-2013 data, but it is 12 trades a year per pair, relies on trading at the fix price (which retail clients cannot generally do), and its sample ends before the 2015 change in the fix methodology and the post-scandal changes in dealer behaviour.

### Gaps
- Ranaldo (2009) magnitudes and any cost analysis were not retrieved.
- No study was found that measures the time-of-day or fix effects with data after 2019, so the 2020-2026 size of the effect is unknown.
- Tokyo-fix specific studies (for example Ito and Yamada on the Tokyo fixing) were not read; the Tokyo numbers above come from Krohn et al.
- Quarter-end effects distinct from month-end were not found quantified.
- No published overnight-versus-intraday return decomposition for major FX pairs was found beyond the session decompositions above.
- No test was found of whether retail traders at typical retail spreads can capture any of these effects; all net results assume interdealer or scaled quoted spreads.

## Key question 3. How quickly did published intraday FX technical profits decay, and what do the most recent samples (2018-2026) show?

### Takeaway
On daily data the decay is well dated: developed-market rule profits were strong in the 1970s, thin in the 1980s, statistically absent from the early 1990s, and around 1% a year net out of sample in 2012-2016. On intraday data the profits were never clearly there net of costs even in 1990s samples; the time-of-day effects persisted for about 15 years and then roughly halved after 2013-2014. Almost nothing rigorous has been published on 2018-2026 intraday samples.

### Cited Findings
- HTW (2016): number of significantly predictive rules for developed currencies falls from five of nine currencies in 1972-76 to "only a few" in the 1980s and "none since the 1992-1996 period"; 2012-2015 out-of-sample developed portfolio 1.35% a year, Sharpe 0.24; the developed-currency rule portfolio shows "a significant falling off in performance after about 2010". Emerging currencies remained profitable (7.3% a year, Sharpe 1.81 in 2012-2015). — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- HTW interpret the decline as the "self-destruction" of profitable rules (Timmermann and Granger) and the adaptive markets hypothesis (Lo; Neely, Weller and Ulrich 2009). — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- Schulmeister: daily yen-dollar technical profits declined from the late 1980s / mid 1990s and "disappeared since 2000"; 30-minute dollar-euro models in 1999-2006 did worse than daily models. — [WIFO WP 325](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wfo/wpaper/y2008i325.html); [WIFO dollar-euro study](https://www.wifo.ac.at/?p=113125)
- Zarrabi et al. (2017), 1994-2014: FDR-selected rules persist for 1 to 3 months out of sample; none is consistently profitable across the whole sample. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v49y2017icp113-127.html)
- Hassanniakalager et al. (2021), 2010-2016: snooping-robust rule portfolios earn 1% a year or less out of sample (2013-2016) after 3 bp per trade. — [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/246954/2/246954.pdf)
- Intraday in the 1990s: Neely-Weller's 1996 break-even costs (0.4 to 3.3 bp one-way) were already at or below institutional costs of 2 to 2.5 bp one-way at the time; Curcio et al. found analysts' filter rules unprofitable on intraday data; Dempster et al. found no significant profits at realistic costs on 1994-1998 GBP/USD. — [Neely-Weller WP](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/1999/1999-016.pdf); [Curcio et al.](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ijf:ijfiec:v:2:y:1997:i:4:p:267-80); [Dempster et al.](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/wp0030.pdf)
- Time-of-day effect over time: Breedon-Ranaldo find the EUR/USD session gap stable every year 1999-2007 except 2004. Krohn et al.: Tokyo-fix reversal returns "have been around 5% or below since 2013, and the first year with a negative reversal return was recorded in 2018"; Europe-fix returns stayed around 10% a year after the crisis "but dropped to below 5% during the last two years" (2018-2019); in the 2014-2019 sub-sample the Tokyo reversal is "notably smaller but remains statistically significant". The authors attribute the decline to arbitrage capital and to low volatility (reversal returns are lower in low-volatility periods). — [SNB WP](https://www.snb.ch/public/asset/en/www-snb-ch/publications/research/working-papers/2011/working_paper_2011_04/publications0_en/working_paper_2011_04.n.pdf); [Bank of Canada WP](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- Krohn et al. net of half the quoted spread: the Tokyo reversal earned negative returns early, "positive but relatively flat returns until around 2007", and "negative returns for the EUR and GBP and flat returns for the JPY" since about 2013. — [Bank of Canada WP](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- Evans: the month-end fix reversal continued to work post-2010 for rules chosen on pre-2010 data (average 3.25% return), on a sample ending 2013. — [MPRA paper](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81583/1/MPRA_paper_81583.pdf)
- Most recent out-of-sample windows found: Tilburg pairs thesis (hourly, Apr 2015-Nov 2021, 16.48% cumulative net); practitioner session tests to Aug 2026 in crypto (net negative). No peer-reviewed intraday FX rule study with a sample ending after 2021 was located. — [Tilburg thesis](http://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=158611); [SimpleMarkets](https://simplemarkets.io/strategies/research-log/opening-range-breakout-costs)
- Prior project finding carried forward (not re-verified here): Neely, Weller and Ulrich (2009) date the disappearance of daily moving-average and filter-rule profits to the early 1990s, and Bartram, Djuranovik, Garratt and Xu (JFQA) report post-publication decay of currency anomalies. HTW cite Neely-Weller-Ulrich to the same effect. — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)

### Inferences
- A workable summary of decay speeds: (i) simple daily trend rules in developed FX, roughly 15 to 20 years from wide use to statistical insignificance (1970s to early 1990s); (ii) FDR-selected rule sets, 1 to 3 months of persistence; (iii) the session/fix drift, stable for about 15 years (1997-2013) and then roughly halved within five years of becoming widely known and traded.
- Since the documented fix reversal is weaker in low-volatility periods and 2024 and 2026 were low-volatility years for EUR/USD (see Key question 6), the effect in 2026 is plausibly smaller still than the "below 5% a year" recorded for 2018-2019. This is an extrapolation, not a measurement.
- Because the published record stops around 2019-2021, any rule tested today on 2022-2026 intraday data is effectively being tested out of sample relative to the literature. That is an opportunity (the hold-out is genuinely unseen by published work) and a warning (there is no published benchmark to confirm a positive result against).

### Gaps
- No rigorous published evidence on intraday technical rules in major FX for 2022 (trend year), August 2024 (yen carry unwind), April 2025 (tariff shock) or the 2026 low-volatility period was found.
- Whether the Bollinger/RSI robustness reported by Coakley et al. for their "more recent sub-sample" survived after June 2015 is unknown.

## Key question 4. Which pairs and sessions are the most favourable for trend-style versus range-style intraday rules, on the evidence?

### Takeaway
The published evidence supports a session map more than a pair ranking: the hours around the Tokyo, Frankfurt and London fixes show systematic drift followed by reversal (favouring fade or session-timed rules), EUR/USD is the only pair where the session effect survived interbank costs, and higher bar frequencies tilt toward mean-reversion while trend rules worked best in emerging or managed currencies and around intervention. Direct tests of range-bound crosses (EUR/CHF, EUR/GBP, AUD/NZD) at intraday frequency were not found.

### Cited Findings
- Session drift directions (1997-2007, mid-quote): EUR/USD falls in the European session and rises in the US session; GBP/USD the same; USD/CHF rises in the Swiss session and falls in the US session; effects in USD/JPY and AUD/USD are much smaller. Net of EBS spreads only EUR/USD was profitable; JPY, CHF, GBP and especially AUD pairs were negative. — [Breedon-Ranaldo, SNB WP](https://www.snb.ch/public/asset/en/www-snb-ch/publications/research/working-papers/2011/working_paper_2011_04/publications0_en/working_paper_2011_04.n.pdf)
- Fix windows (New York time): USD strengthens 5:00-8:55 pm into the Tokyo fix and weakens 8:55 pm-2:00 am; strengthens 2:00-8:15 am into the ECB fix and weakens from the London fix (11:00 am) to 5:00 pm. The yen is the exception around the European fixes (it appreciates in the post-London window, so the trade loses). — [Krohn et al., Bank of Canada WP](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- Fix reversal strength varies with volatility and dealer risk-bearing capacity: yearly reversal returns were about 20% in 2001 and during the 2008 crisis and lowest in calm periods (Tokyo 2004-2007, Europe 2007, both after 2013). — [Krohn et al., Bank of Canada WP](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- Month-end London-fix reversal by pair (2004-2013, 15-minute horizon, annual % / Sharpe): strongest in AUD/USD (9.19 / 3.38), EUR/GBP (8.33 / 2.87), NZD/EUR (10.70 / 3.69), DKK/USD (8.21 / 3.18); negative in USD/GBP (-3.05 / -1.18), JPY/USD (-1.65 / -0.75), CAD/GBP (-4.73 / -1.35). — [Evans, MPRA paper Table 6](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81583/1/MPRA_paper_81583.pdf)
- Frequency and rule style: in high-frequency commodity ETF data mean-reversion rules improve and trend-following rules deteriorate as sampling frequency rises. — [Hudson, McGroarty and Urquhart 2017](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/404223/). In FX, 30-minute moving-average/momentum models did worse than daily ones on dollar-euro 1999-2006. — [Schulmeister, WIFO](https://www.wifo.ac.at/?p=113125)
- Trend rules and currency type: after 2012 daily technical rules earned Sharpe 1.81 in emerging currencies versus 0.24 in developed ones; HTW also report that mean excess returns of the best rules rise with exchange-rate volatility (a 100 bp rise in annualised daily volatility is associated with a 417 bp rise in the best rule's annualised mean excess return) while Sharpe ratios do not. — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- Intervention regimes: LeBaron (1999, Journal of International Economics 49:125-143) linked moving-average rule profits to days of central-bank intervention; Neely (2002, Journal of International Economics 58:211-232) used high-frequency data to show that abnormally high trading-rule returns precede German, Swiss and US intervention, so intervention responds to the trends the rules were already riding and does not create the profits. — [Neely 2002 record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/inecon/v58y2002i1p211-232.html); [St. Louis Fed WP 2000-018](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:fip:fedlwp:2000-018)
- Managed-rate regime at high frequency: on RUB/USD, rules "applied at a sufficiently high frequency generate superior returns when the central bank conducts a stabilizing exchange rate policy", though real transaction costs remove most profits. — [Frömmel and Lampaert 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finlet/v18y2016icp177-183.html)
- Range-style rule families in large rule sets: Bollinger band and RSI rules were the ones reported as still robust across 22 currencies in the later part of 1996-2015 (daily). — [Coakley et al. 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v45y2016icp273-282.html)
- Hourly cointegration pairs across 26 FX pairs: positive net out of sample 2015-2021; results improved when CHF and JPY pairs were removed. — [Tilburg thesis](http://arno.uvt.nl/show.cgi?fid=158611)
- Tail-risk regimes relevant to range trading: on 15 Jan 2015 the SNB removed the 1.20 EUR/CHF floor and the euro fell as much as 41% against the franc intraday; FXCM reported about $225 million of client negative balances and needed a $300 million rescue loan; Alpari (UK) entered insolvency; Interactive Brokers' client losses were put at about $120 million. — [CRS Insight](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20150211_IN10230_fbd6178a37df59924423af5f8ec3545533730cb8.html); [Swissinfo](https://www.swissinfo.ch/eng/fxcm-faces-losses-as-swiss-shock-leaves-alpari-uk-insolvent/41219974); [Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/brokers/interactive-brokers-suffers-120-million-client-losses-trading-uninterrupted/)
- August 2024: the BIS describes "significant volatility in the first days of August 2024" with yen-funded carry trades hardest hit, while noting FX volatility rose less than equity volatility. — [BIS Bulletin 90](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull90.pdf); [BIS Quarterly Review Sept 2024](https://bis.org/publications/qr-202409/carry-off-carry-on)
- April 2025: FX turnover hit a record $9.5 trillion a day (Triennial Survey) amid tariff announcements, an unexpected dollar depreciation and a rush to hedge; spot turnover was up 42% on 2022; dealer-customer bid-ask spreads stayed "rather resilient" and price impact was lower than in March 2020. — [BIS Quarterly Review Dec 2025](https://www.bis.org/publ/qtrpdf/r_qt2512b.htm)
- 2026: the BIS reports a sharp yen recovery on 31 July 2026 after joint official intervention by Japan's finance ministry and the US Treasury. — [BIS Quarterly Review Sept 2026](https://www.bis.org/publications/qr-202609/yields-climb-yet-risk-appetite-holds-firm)
- Volatility regimes by year for EUR/USD and USD/JPY (average daily range and realised volatility, 2015-2026) are tabulated under Key question 6. — [Alpha Vantage daily FX data, Vendor](https://www.alphavantage.co/query?function=FX_DAILY&from_symbol=EUR&to_symbol=USD&outputsize=full&apikey=demo)

### Inferences
- Trend-style intraday rules have the best documented footing where there is a known directional order-flow imbalance with a clock: USD strength into each fix, base-currency weakness in its home session, and (historically) pre-intervention trends and managed emerging currencies. Range/fade-style rules have the best footing immediately after those same events (post-fix reversal) and at round numbers (take-profit clustering).
- For a retail trader restricted to liquid majors, EUR/USD in the European morning and the London-fix-to-New-York-close window is the single best-evidenced pair/session combination, because it is the only one where the effect exceeded interbank costs. GBP/USD shows the same direction but did not survive costs in 1997-2007; USD/JPY behaves differently around the European fixes and should not be assumed to mirror EUR/USD.
- Range-bound crosses (EUR/CHF, EUR/GBP, AUD/NZD) are attractive for mean-reversion in principle because both legs share regional drivers, but (a) their spreads are wider in bp terms, (b) the 2015 SNB event shows that a "range" maintained by policy can end in a gap that stops cannot control, and (c) the one pairs study improved when CHF and JPY pairs were dropped. A sensible conclusion is to test range rules on EUR/GBP and AUD/NZD with explicit gap-risk stress and to treat any policy-anchored cross as carrying an unpriced tail.
- Volatility conditioning cuts both ways: HTW find higher volatility raises mean returns of trend rules but not Sharpe ratios; Krohn et al. find fix reversals are larger in high-volatility years. In the low-volatility conditions of 2024 and 2026, both the size of moves and the documented effects shrink while costs do not, which argues for fewer trades and for range-style logic over breakout logic, and the reverse in years like 2022 and 2025. This is an inference from regime evidence, not a tested rule.
- An ADX-type or realised-volatility filter has no direct published test in intraday FX found here; the case for it rests on the regime dependence above.

### Gaps
- No intraday study of Bollinger/RSI or other range rules specifically on EUR/CHF, EUR/GBP or AUD/NZD was found. "No formal evidence found."
- No published test of ADX-style trend filters, realised-volatility filters, or carry/risk-off regime filters applied to intraday FX technical rules was found.
- LeBaron (1999) was confirmed only at citation level in this session; its headline numbers were not re-extracted.
- No rigorous study of intraday technical-rule performance specifically during the 2022 trend year, the August 2024 carry unwind or the 2025-2026 period was found.
- Session-by-session realised volatility and range shares (Asia/London/New York) for 2024-2026 were not obtained.

## Key question 5. What is the minimum sound testing protocol implied by this literature, and how many trades are needed to distinguish a small edge from zero at a given win rate and payoff ratio?

### Takeaway
The literature implies six non-negotiables: executable bid/ask prices with time-varying spreads, a correction for every rule and parameter set tried, an untouched recent hold-out, periodic re-selection (walk-forward), cost stress up to at least twice the base case, and enough trades for power. A realistic intraday edge of 0.04R to 0.10R per trade needs on the order of 600 to 4,000 trades for a single pre-specified rule at conventional significance and 80% power, and roughly 1,500 to 9,000 if the hurdle is raised to t = 3 to allow for multiple testing.

### Cited Findings
- **Use tradable prices, not mid-quotes.** Breedon-Ranaldo's six-pair result flips from "significant for all pairs" at mid to "profitable for EUR/USD only" at firm bid/ask. Krohn et al.'s EUR Tokyo trade goes from +10.44% (mid) to +2.65% (half spread) to -5.14% (full quoted spread). Indicative quoted spreads can overstate effective institutional spreads by up to 75%, and a retail aggregator's spread was about twice the EBS inside spread. — [Breedon-Ranaldo](https://www.snb.ch/public/asset/en/www-snb-ch/publications/research/working-papers/2011/working_paper_2011_04/publications0_en/working_paper_2011_04.n.pdf); [Krohn et al.](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf); [Evans](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81583/1/MPRA_paper_81583.pdf)
- **Train with costs in the objective.** Neely-Weller rules evolved at zero cost traded about once an hour and broke even at 0.4 to 1.0 bp; rules evolved with 2 bp cost traded far less and broke even at 2.1 to 3.3 bp. Dempster et al. report the same pattern across 1, 4 and 8 bp slippage and stress that constraining in-sample learning reduces overfitting. — [Neely-Weller WP](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/1999/1999-016.pdf); [Dempster et al.](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/wp0030.pdf)
- **Report break-even cost per rule** (the one-way cost that sets excess return to zero) and compare it with actual cost, as in Neely-Weller and HTW; HTW's best developed-market rules had break-even costs 6.6 to 43 times actual cost because they traded rarely. — [HTW SSRN copy](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- **Correct for the whole search.** Coakley et al.: best rules up to 30% a year before correction, "sharp fall" in robust rules after Step-SPA. Hassanniakalager et al.: 5% to 15% of 7,846 rules pass a Romano-Wolf type test in sample, yet the resulting portfolios earn 1% a year or less out of sample. Bajgrowicz and Scaillet (2012, Journal of Financial Economics 106:473-491) introduced the FDR approach with persistence tests and found in-sample performance "completely offset by the introduction of low transaction costs" (Dow Jones index data). — [Coakley et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v45y2016icp273-282.html); [Hassanniakalager et al.](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/246954/2/246954.pdf); [Bajgrowicz and Scaillet](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v106y2012i3p473-491.html)
- **Three-way split and a genuinely unseen hold-out.** Neely-Weller use training / selection / test periods. HTW select on data to end-2011 and evaluate on 2012-Sept 2015 and call this "an extremely stringent out-of-sample evaluation". Evans re-estimates on pre-2010 data and trades post-2010. — [Neely-Weller WP](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/1999/1999-016.pdf); [HTW](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf); [Evans](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81583/1/MPRA_paper_81583.pdf)
- **Walk-forward with short re-selection windows.** Zarrabi et al. re-select by FDR each month and hold one month; persistence is 1 to 3 months. Hassanniakalager et al. roll 3 years in sample / 1 year out. Dempster et al. use a moving window. — [Zarrabi et al.](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v49y2017icp113-127.html); [Hassanniakalager et al.](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/246954/2/246954.pdf); [Dempster et al.](https://www.jbs.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/wp0030.pdf)
- **Respect trading hours and liquidity.** Neely-Weller's break-even costs fall when trading is confined to a 12-hour high-volume window; their 24-hour, 7-day assumption flattered results. — [Neely-Weller WP](https://s3.amazonaws.com/real.stlouisfed.org/wp/1999/1999-016.pdf)
- **Power is low in short samples.** HTW: the approximate t-ratio is the annualised Sharpe ratio times the square root of years; with 3.7 years, a Sharpe of 0.85 is needed for significance at even the 10% one-sided level; a rule with true Sharpe 0.6 needs (1.64/0.6)^2 = 7.5 years. They also cite Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016) on the need for out-of-sample validation. — [HTW SSRN copy, footnote 34](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf)
- **Pre-registration and independent windows** are used in the better practitioner work: hypotheses fixed in advance, two non-overlapping windows, pass only if net positive in both, survives removal of the best trade, and drawdown within a preset limit; that same log documents how an inappropriate fee assumption and an unintended overnight-hold convention changed conclusions. — [SimpleMarkets, Practitioner](https://simplemarkets.io/strategies/research-log/opening-range-breakout-costs)
- **Null model matters.** Osler tests published levels against artificial levels; Ben Omrane-Van Oppens use Monte Carlo; Sokolovski finds an hourly rule adds nothing beyond a seasonal GARCH null. — [Osler 2000](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/epr/00v06n2/0007osle.pdf); [Ben Omrane-Van Oppens](https://ideas.repec.org/p/cor/louvco/2004035.html); [Sokolovski](https://open.uct.ac.za/bitstreams/1ca4b008-7ebc-4b87-8804-c8674358c228/download)

### Inferences

**Minimum protocol (synthesis of the above)**
1. Data: bid and ask series (not mid) at the bar frequency traded, from the venue or broker actually used, including the rollover hour and news minutes; model fills at the touch plus commission plus a slippage allowance.
2. Rule log: count every rule family, parameter value, pair, session filter and stop/target variant ever evaluated. That count is the multiple-testing denominator.
3. Splits: training, selection and a final hold-out of the most recent 18 to 24 months that is opened once. Given the literature ends around 2019-2021, holding out 2025-2026 also tests the current low-volatility regime.
4. Snooping control: White's reality check or Hansen's SPA on the best rule; Romano-Wolf stepwise or FDR when a set of rules is to be kept. If none of these is implemented, require t >= 3 on net per-trade returns as a blunt substitute.
5. Walk-forward: re-select on a rolling window (for example 2 to 3 years) and trade the next 1 to 3 months, since documented persistence of selected rules is 1 to 3 months.
6. Costs: base case at measured spread plus commission; stress at 1.5 times and 2 times; report break-even cost per rule and reject rules whose break-even is under twice the base cost.
7. Robustness: results by year and by volatility regime, with and without the best 1% of trades, neighbouring parameter values, a second pair not used in selection, and exclusion of the five minutes around scheduled news and the rollover hour.
8. Null comparison: randomised entry times with the same holding period and stop/target (to check the edge is not just the session drift or the stop geometry).
9. Power: decide the required trade count before running (below).

**Worked estimate: trades needed**

Set-up [Own calc]: each trade wins +b R with probability p or loses 1 R with probability (1 - p), net of costs, where R is the amount risked. Expectancy E = p*b - (1 - p). Per-trade standard deviation s = sqrt(p*b^2 + (1 - p) - E^2). Per-trade Sharpe = E/s. Trades needed N = (z / (E/s))^2, where z = 2.0 for a bare "t = 2" (only 50% power), z = 1.645 + 0.842 = 2.49 for a one-sided 5% test with 80% power, and z = 3.0 + 0.842 = 3.84 for a t = 3 hurdle (a common multiple-testing allowance) with 80% power. Trades are assumed independent; positive serial correlation or overlapping positions raise N.

| Win rate p | Payoff b | Expectancy E | Per-trade Sharpe | N for t = 2 | N for 5% one-sided, 80% power | N for t = 3, 80% power |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 52% | 1.0 | +0.04R | 0.040 | 2,496 | 3,860 | 9,211 |
| 50% | 1.1 | +0.05R | 0.048 | 1,764 | 2,728 | 6,510 |
| 35% | 2.0 | +0.05R | 0.035 | 3,276 | 5,066 | 12,089 |
| 55% | 1.0 | +0.10R | 0.101 | 396 | 612 | 1,461 |
| 45% | 1.5 | +0.125R | 0.101 | 396 | 612 | 1,461 |
| 40% | 2.0 | +0.20R | 0.136 | 216 | 334 | 797 |
| 60% | 1.0 | +0.20R | 0.204 | 96 | 148 | 354 |

Reading the table: a 52% win rate at 1:1 after costs (the kind of edge the literature suggests is the realistic upper range for a mechanical intraday rule) needs about 3,900 trades to detect with 80% power, which is 8 to 15 years at one or two trades a day on one pair, or 2 to 4 years across four reasonably independent pair/session slots. A 55% win rate at 1:1 needs about 600 trades (roughly 1.5 to 2.5 years at one or two trades a day). Low win-rate, high payoff designs need more trades for the same expectancy because per-trade variance is higher (compare rows 2 and 3). An apparent 60% at 1:1 on 150 trades is exactly what the best of a few dozen tried variants of a zero-edge rule can produce, which is why the t = 3 column is the safer planning number when more than a handful of variants were examined.

- This is consistent with HTW's own power arithmetic (Sharpe 0.6 needs 7.5 years) and explains why sub-period tests in that paper found nothing after 1992 even though modest edges could not be excluded.
- Break-even cost should be reported in the same units as the edge: if the measured gross edge is 0.05R and the cost is 0.03R, the rule is one spread-widening away from zero, whatever its t-statistic.

### Gaps
- No FX-specific published guidance on minimum trade counts was found; the table is a standard power calculation, not a literature result.
- The size of serial correlation in intraday FX rule returns (which would inflate the required N) was not found quantified.
- No consensus in the literature on which snooping test is best for intraday rule sets with overlapping signals; the papers above use different ones (Step-SPA, Romano-Wolf, FDR).

## Key question 6. What do average intraday ranges and retail spreads imply about the largest trading frequency that can plausibly be profitable (why 1-minute scalping is ruled out but one or two trades per session might not be)?

### Takeaway
In 2026 EUR/USD moved about 65 pips (about 56 bp) high-to-low on an average day, with realised volatility near 5.5% to 5.7%; that puts the typical 1-minute move at about 1 bp and the typical 1-hour move at about 7 bp. With an all-in round-trip cost of 1 bp the directional accuracy needed just to break even is above 100% at 1-minute holds, about 80% at 5 minutes, 67% at 15 minutes, 59% at 1 hour and 53% to 54% at 4 to 8 hours, so only holds of an hour or more (one or two trades a session) leave a requirement within the range the literature has ever documented.

### Inferences

**Cost per round trip in practice [Own calc]**
- IBKR-style agency pricing on EUR/USD at 1.15: spread 0.1 to 0.3 pip (0.09 to 0.26 bp) plus 2 x 0.2 bp commission = about 0.5 to 0.7 bp round trip, but only if each order is large enough that the per-order minimum does not bind. At a USD 2 minimum the commission equals 0.2 bp only at USD 100,000 notional; at USD 25,000 notional it is 0.8 bp per side (1.6 bp round trip from commission alone) and at USD 10,000 it is 2 bp per side. For a small account the minimum ticket fee, not the spread, sets the cost floor, and a spread-only dealer at about 0.6 pip (0.5 bp) can be cheaper.
- Spread-only retail dealer: about 0.6 to 1.0 pip on EUR/USD = about 0.5 to 0.9 bp round trip in normal hours, more with slippage on stop orders; 1 to 2 bp round trip is a fair planning range for EUR/USD and 2 to 4 bp for crosses.

**Why frequency is capped [Own calc]**
Assume returns over a holding period h are roughly symmetric with standard deviation sd_h = daily sd x sqrt(h / 1440 minutes), mean absolute move about 0.8 x sd_h, and a rule that is right about direction with probability p and earns or loses the typical move. Gross expectancy per trade is (2p - 1) x 0.8 x sd_h, so the break-even accuracy is p* = 0.5 + c / (1.6 x sd_h) for round-trip cost c. Daily sd is 35.7 bp at 5.67% annual volatility (2026) and 49.3 bp at 7.83% (2025).

| Holding period | Typical move (sd), 2026 vol | Break-even accuracy at c = 0.5 bp | at c = 1 bp | at c = 2 bp | Break-even at c = 1 bp, 2025 vol |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 minute | 0.9 bp | 83% | impossible (>100%) | impossible | 98% |
| 5 minutes | 2.1 bp | 65% | 80% | impossible | 72% |
| 15 minutes | 3.6 bp | 59% | 67% | 84% | 62% |
| 1 hour | 7.3 bp | 54% | 59% | 67% | 56% |
| 4 hours | 14.6 bp | 52% | 54% | 59% | 53% |
| 8 hours (one session) | 20.6 bp | 51.5% | 53% | 56% | 52% |
| 1 day | 35.7 bp | 51% | 52% | 53.5% | 51% |

- Volatility is not uniform across the day (higher in the London morning and the London-New York overlap), so active-hour moves are somewhat larger than this table and overnight moves smaller; the ordering does not change.
- Comparison with what has been documented: the largest replicated intraday edges are 2.5 to 4 bp per multi-hour window (time-of-day/fix effects) and the largest per-trade break-even costs for machine-selected hourly rules were 2 to 3 bp one-way in 1996. Nothing in the literature supports sustained directional accuracy of 65% to 80% on 5- to 15-minute holds, which is what a 1 bp round trip demands there. Hence 1-minute and 5-minute scalping is ruled out at retail costs by arithmetic, 15-minute holds are marginal even at 0.5 bp, and holds of one hour to one session are the shortest horizon at which the required accuracy (53% to 59%) is within sight of documented effects.

**Stops, targets and break-even win rate [Own calc]**
With stop S pips, target T pips and round-trip cost c pips, break-even win rate = (S + c) / (S + T).

| Stop / target (pips) | No cost | c = 0.5 pip | c = 1.2 pips (about 1 bp) | c = 2.3 pips (about 2 bp) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 / 5 | 50.0% | 55.0% | 62.0% | 73.0% |
| 10 / 10 | 50.0% | 52.5% | 56.0% | 61.5% |
| 10 / 20 | 33.3% | 35.0% | 37.3% | 41.0% |
| 20 / 20 | 50.0% | 51.2% | 53.0% | 55.7% |
| 20 / 40 | 33.3% | 34.2% | 35.3% | 37.2% |
| 30 / 60 | 33.3% | 33.9% | 34.7% | 35.9% |

- Cost adds c / (S + T) to the break-even win rate, so the damage is inversely proportional to trade size in pips. With a 2026 median daily range of 58 pips, a 20/40 trade already asks for about two thirds of a typical day's range in one direction; a 30/60 trade asks for more than the median day. In a 57-pip-range regime (second half of 2026) the feasible design space is roughly 10 to 25 pip stops with 15 to 40 pip targets, one or two trades per session, which is exactly where cost adds 1 to 6 percentage points to the break-even win rate.
- Frequency ceiling as a budget: at two round trips a day and 1 bp each, annual cost is about 5% of notional (2 x 1 bp x 252); at ten a day it is about 25%. For comparison the best documented gross session effect was 8% to 16% a year on notional in 1999-2014 and under 5% in 2018-2019. Two trades a day spends roughly the whole recent gross effect on costs unless cost is nearer 0.5 bp; ten trades a day cannot be funded by any documented effect.
- Rollover hour (5 pm New York) and the first Tokyo hour have the widest spreads; a session-based rule should neither enter nor let stops rest unprotected across those windows without modelling the wider spread, and backtests on mid or on a constant spread will overstate results most for rules that trade the Asian range.
- Regulator loss statistics (about three quarters of retail CFD accounts lose) are consistent with this arithmetic: typical retail turnover is far above what the cost-to-range ratio allows.
